Comparative essay

Where Manufacturing Happens: Concentration and Distributed Work in Six Objects

Introduction

A finished object offers a misleadingly simple answer to where manufacturing happens: the place where its parts finally come together. This project challenges that answer through six historical snapshots: a nail around 1650, cloth in 1835, canned meat in 1901, a Ford Model A in 1928, an RCA television in 1965, and an iPhone in 2007. Its argument is that concentrating production repeatedly reorganized geographically distributed work rather than eliminating it. Transport enabled connections, but corporate ownership, specialized capabilities, and decisions about coordination shaped their organization. The resulting atlas therefore questions both the self-sufficient factory and an inevitable progression from local production to globalization.

Background

The course lecture American Empire and Industrialization by Marco Gabriell Muñoz connects industrialization to regional inequality and concentrated capital (Muñoz, 2026, Lecture 4, slides 4, 6–7). Its discussion of northern investment in southern industry distinguishes the location of productive activity from the location of economic control; its Gilded Age discussion connects industrial expansion to changes in work and wealth. The atlas develops that distinction at the scale of individual products. Its maps separate raw materials, processing, assembly, and corporate locations. These are analytical categories, not interchangeable dots. A generated component study explains selected structural relationships, while a geographical map asks where documented tasks occurred. Neither visual device independently establishes a historical supply chain. The comparison also draws on Lecture 3’s discussion of sectional identity and slavery (slides 4–6), Lecture 5’s account of automobiles and manufacturing in the 1920s (slide 10), and Lecture 6’s treatment of globalization (slide 19). These lectures establish the historical questions; outside sources establish the selected objects’ production arrangements.

Argument

Early production already combined concentrated processing with dispersed work. At Saugus, iron passed through a furnace, forge, and rolling and slitting mill. Most nail rods were shipped to Boston and other New England settlements, although some became nails locally (National Park Service, 2021). The nail around 1650 thus anchors a colonial production system, not a claim about a traceable surviving specimen. Its significance lies in the distinction between making a standardized intermediate material and finishing a usable object. A specialized works could concentrate demanding operations while supplying other producers. Local manufacture did not require every stage to remain local.

The 1835 cloth case shows that geographical separation could also conceal social dependence. Lowell Manufacturing Company produced inexpensive cloth for sale to enslavers, while Lowell mills relied on cotton produced through enslaved labor (National Park Service, 2020). The northern factory and southern plantation consequently belonged to connected economic relationships. This does not establish a closed circuit in which one identifiable batch of cotton returned as clothing. It establishes a narrower but consequential point: a northern manufacturing location did not make production independent of southern coercion. Here, identifying the source of raw material changes the interpretation of the finished object.

The canned-meat case shifts the question from separated stages to concentrated processing. A catalog records a 1901 film of can labeling at Armour's Chicago operation (American Film Institute, n.d.); this is evidence of a documented activity, not a basis for reconstructing unseen footage. Chicago's stockyards brought livestock by rail into an urban processing system with specialized jobs (Chicago History Museum, n.d.). Rail connections helped make concentration possible, but a junction alone does not explain why production accumulated there. Klein and Crafts (2009) find that market potential, interacting with scale economies and industrial linkages, helps explain the manufacturing belt. Their regional analysis suggests a competing mechanism: factories could benefit from access to customers and other producers as well as incoming materials. It does not independently prove Armour's specific location decisions.

The Model A illustrates why factory concentration must be distinguished from territorial self-sufficiency. A dated photograph records Model A assembly at Ford's Rouge complex in February 1928 (Ford Motor Company, Photographic Department, 1928). The Henry Ford's railroad history explains that Rouge obtained coal from Kentucky and West Virginia and connected external transport with internal materials handling (The Henry Ford, n.d.). The important boundary was therefore organizational as well as geographical. Bringing operations into a large complex did not bring their resource base inside its walls. Nor does the complex's later development establish that every component was already manufactured there in 1928.

The RCA and iPhone cases reveal a further distinction between distributing work across company plants and coordinating external manufacturers. A 1965 industry report identifies RCA's tube facilities at Marion and Lancaster, receiver production at Bloomington, and components at Indianapolis. It also describes proposed expansion, which must not be mistaken for completed capacity (Broadcasting, 1965, pp. 28–29). Apple's 2007 annual report, by contrast, places final assembly of substantially all its portable products, explicitly including the iPhone, with third-party vendors in China (Apple Inc., 2007, pp. 12, 16–17). These primary sources show different organizational arrangements without requiring an unsupported factory location for every component.

Reading those two sources together also reveals how evidence shapes the apparent geography of production. The trade report presents RCA's network through expansion plans and anticipated demand; Apple's legally required disclosure emphasizes dependence and disruption risk (Broadcasting, 1965, pp. 28–29; Apple Inc., 2007, pp. 16–17). The RCA forecast is “entirely dependent upon” picture-tube availability (p. 28), making projected expansion conditional on a specific component. Apple, meanwhile, says outsourcing arrangements “reduce the Company’s direct control” (p. 16), placing a governance cost alongside potential savings. Expansion and vulnerability are not opposite conditions: both presuppose coordination among specialized locations. Yet their different purposes prevent a simple comparison of corporate success. Gereffi et al. (2003) explain why coordination depends on transaction complexity, the ability to codify information, and supplier capabilities. This framework sharpens the comparison beyond “American” versus “foreign” manufacturing. It does not authorize assigning every Apple relationship to one governance category from a company address or a component label.

Counterarguments

A reasonable objection is that transport improvement provides a simpler explanation for the atlas's widening connections. Transport matters, but it cannot alone explain why operations cluster or why firms own some stages and outsource others. Klein and Crafts (2009) distinguish market access and industrial linkages from natural advantages, while Gereffi et al. (2003) show that similar geographical separation can accommodate different forms of coordination. Together, these explanations make distance a condition to investigate rather than a sufficient cause. The six cases also form a selective comparison, not a representative sample from which to calculate national manufacturing shares.

Continuity across the cases should not erase historical change. A colonial nail-rod network and an electronics supply chain differ substantially in technology, scale, institutions, and the tasks being coordinated. The claim is therefore about a recurring relationship between concentration and distribution, not an unchanged system. A dated epilogue makes its continuing relevance visible: Apple's 2025 report states that changing its supply chain requires considerable time and resources (Apple Inc., 2025, Item 1A). That contemporary company-level statement supports the importance of established relationships; it supplies no missing evidence about the 2007 iPhone's component origins.

Conclusion

The atlas's contribution is to make a factory location the beginning of historical explanation rather than its endpoint. Across six objects, concentrated operations appear within wider arrangements of materials, labor, transport, and authority. Comparing those arrangements connects small material objects to the course's larger questions about regional development and capital. The maps and diagrams support that argument only when they distinguish documented connections from schematic reconstruction. Manufacturing geography becomes most revealing when it shows both what a place does and what that activity depends upon elsewhere.

Exhibition text

The following six texts match the website. Source codes refer to the indexed bibliography below.

1650 — The iron nail

At Saugus in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, concentrated iron processing supplied work beyond the ironworks. Workers smelted bog ore with charcoal and a mineral flux brought from Nahant. Refining produced wrought-iron bars; rolling and slitting turned some of those bars into nail rods. [S1, S3, S6]

Most rods went to Boston and other New England settlements, although some nails were made at Saugus. The intermediate material could therefore leave the works before becoming a finished object. Water supported both mechanical power and transport, while English investment connected the works to an Atlantic commercial world. [S1, S7]

The nail represents a production network around 1650, before the United States existed. It makes the division between concentrated iron processing and dispersed finishing visible; no surviving specimen is traced through that network.

Course connection. Lecture 2, pp. 12–13: British colonies and commercial ties. Saugus supplies an independently researched material example. Lecture 1, pp. 25–26 informs the distinction between mapped evidence and inference.

1835 — Cotton cloth

Lowell's northern mills depended on southern cotton produced through enslaved labor. Waterpower and investment concentrated processing in Massachusetts, while merchants and transport connected the mills to a wider economy. The Middlesex Canal carried materials and goods; the Boston and Lowell Railroad added a connection in 1835. [L1, L3, L6, L7]

An 1835 company table lists coarse clothing material among Lowell Manufacturing Company's products. The National Park Service identifies sales of such cloth to enslavers. These sources establish an economic connection between northern manufacturing and the plantation economy, without tracing one batch from cotton field to finished clothing. [L2, L3]

Regional difference thus coexisted with dependence. The factory's location cannot separate its history from the coercion involved in supplying its raw material. The illustration follows fiber, yarn and woven cloth; it does not establish the exact composition of every textile in the company table.

Course connection. Lecture 3, slides 4–6: sectional identity and slavery. Lecture 4, slide 4 offers a later comparison with southern textile industrialization; it is not evidence about Lowell in 1835.

1901 — Canned beef

Chicago's meatpacking industry concentrated processing where railways brought livestock from the Midwest. Around the Union Stock Yard, specialized work converted animals into meat and packaged products. Transport connected the processing center to both its material supply and distant customers. [M3, M4]

Armour Corned Beef appeared in a Vacaville, California newspaper advertisement in 1893. An AFI catalog entry records can labeling at Armour's Chicago plant in 1901. The advertisement establishes a retail market; the catalog establishes a documented industrial activity. Neither traces the journey of an individual can. [M1, M2]

Schwaab's 1890 handbook describes cooking, deboning and packing corned beef. It supports the process explanation at industry level, without supplying an Armour factory recipe. Canning and refrigerated fresh-beef transport were distinct preservation systems. The can brings the relationship between regional livestock, urban processing and national markets into view. [M5, p. 113]

Course connection. Lecture 4, slides 6–7: rail expansion, concentrated capital and industrial labor. Slide 15 supplies the later context of food regulation; the 1901 case predates those reforms.

1928 — The Model A

A photograph dated February 8, 1928 records Model A assembly at Ford's Rouge complex. It anchors the case to a specific production arrangement, after Rouge had previously supplied Model T engines and parts for assembly elsewhere. [F1, F5]

Rouge depended on resources beyond Dearborn. Coal came from Kentucky and West Virginia; Great Lakes resources and wood processing in Michigan's Upper Peninsula belonged to a wider network. Ships and railways connected external supplies with the complex, and rail transport also moved materials within it. Iron Mountain's wooden body parts passed through other Ford suppliers. [F3, F4]

Assembly brought several production streams together. It did not make the factory territorially self-sufficient. Dates limit the reconstruction: Ford made safety glass at Rouge by 1930, so that arrangement cannot be assigned to every 1928 car. The illustrated body, frame, engine and wheels explain major systems rather than a verified factory assembly order. [F5, F7]

Course connection. Lecture 5, slide 10: automobiles and manufacturing in the 1920s. Slide 12 places northern industrial jobs within the Great Migration; it does not identify the workers in the photographed assembly line.

1965 — Color television

In June 1965, Broadcasting described RCA's expanding color-television operations. Bloomington made receivers; Indianapolis supplied components; Marion and Lancaster made picture tubes. One finished set depended on specialized production across several American cities. [R1, pp. 28–29]

The report also exposed a constraint: suitable picture tubes limited the number of sets the industry could supply. Moving from round to rectangular tubes required investment and production adjustments. Additional assembly capacity alone could not resolve that problem. Forecasts and announced expansion remain distinct from completed capacity. [R1, pp. 28–29]

The cabinet, picture tube, electronic chassis and speaker make these functions visible. Their separation is interpretive: the sources do not identify a factory for every part of this illustrated cabinet. The map records selected RCA plant roles. It supports comparison with Apple's coordination of external manufacturers, while providing a different kind of geographical evidence. [R1, R2]

Course connection. Lecture 6, slide 8: postwar suburban growth and changing regional opportunities. RCA's particular factory network comes from outside research; the lecture does not establish these plant assignments.

2007 — The first iPhone

The original iPhone's packaging identifies California as the design region and China as the assembly country. Apple's 2007 annual report confirms third-party assembly in China for its portable products, including the iPhone. These sources establish different contributions to one product without identifying a specific assembly plant. [I1, I4]

The 2007 8GB teardown, consulted in a later-revised version, identifies specialized components. Paired with company records, it supports an optional map of supplier organizations in South Korea, Germany, Britain and California. Those locations are offices or organizational centers, not established factories for the components inside this phone. [I3, I6–I9]

The internal structure joins a bonded front display to an upper logic board, a battery below it with soldered leads, and a rear enclosure. The illustration preserves those relationships. The case shows how an American firm's product organization could coordinate specialized companies across borders. Later repair guidance informs the illustration; later supply-chain disclosures are dated separately. [I3, I10]

Course connection. Lecture 6, slide 19: globalization and the growing importance of internet devices. The 2007 phone extends that context through independently sourced evidence; a separately dated 2025 epilogue provides the present-day connection.

Source analysis

The argument depends on comparing what sources can establish, as well as what they leave unresolved. The following examples are part of the research method, rather than a claim to have consulted every original record in the referenced archives.

A product table and a historical interpretation

The 1835 Lowell company table supplies contemporary product categories. The National Park Service's Southern Ties & Northern Gospels supplies the interpretation of raw-material dependence and sales to enslavers. Keeping these functions separate prevents the table from being made to answer questions it does not record. Their conjunction supports an argument about interregional dependence; it does not identify a closed route for an individual bale or piece of cloth. [L2, L3]

An advertisement and an industrial catalog record

The Vacaville advertisement of 28 January 1893 is a contemporary attempt to sell goods to a local audience. Its evidentiary value here is the named product's presence in a distant retail market. It provides no shipment itinerary or sales total. The AFI entry for Labeling Cans records a 1901 film associated with Armour's Chicago operation. The catalog was consulted; the film was not viewed. It would therefore be misleading to present a detailed account of workers' movements as an observation from the film. [M1, M2]

A company photograph

Ford's Photographic Department recorded a Model A assembly line at Rouge on 8 February 1928. The image and catalog identify an activity, location and date. They do not by themselves reveal the origins of every component or the workers' personal histories. External supply relationships therefore depend on additional material in The Henry Ford's railroad and factory histories. This separation is essential to the claim that concentrated assembly remained dependent on distant resources. [F1, F3, F5]

Expansion reporting and corporate risk disclosure

Broadcasting reported RCA's color-television expansion on 21 June 1965 through an industry perspective attentive to demand, production and executive forecasts. The report locates specialized activities but also communicates expectations. Apple's 2007 Form 10-K addresses investors through a legally required account of its business and risks. Its discussion of third-party manufacturing makes dependency visible in a different way. Reading the documents together supports a comparison of coordination arrangements; it cannot establish that their maps measure the same kind of facility or that one firm was more successful. [R1; I4]

The RCA report qualifies its industry sales forecast as “entirely dependent upon” picture-tube availability (p. 28). The dependency clause makes a component constraint central to an optimistic expansion story: projected sales are conditional, not an observed result. Apple's 2007 report pairs possible cost savings with “reduce the Company’s direct control” (p. 16). That wording identifies a trade-off within outsourcing rather than treating international distance itself as the risk. Read together, the passages support the argument that specialized production requires coordination, while retaining the distinction between a forecast and a risk disclosure. [R1; I4]

Secondary research as a competing explanation

Klein and Crafts's 2009 working paper examines industrial location through market potential, scale economies and industrial linkages. It challenges a single-cause transport story, although its regional model cannot reconstruct Armour's own decisions. Gereffi, Humphrey and Sturgeon's 2003 manuscript examines how transaction complexity, codification and supplier capabilities shape coordination. It makes the distinction between RCA and Apple more precise, while leaving individual relationships unclassified when evidence is insufficient. Cowie's Capital Moves introduction adds labor and relocation to the RCA discussion; the recorded reading covers the introduction, not the entire book.

These secondary works are used for their explanatory alternatives and limits. Their full bibliographic entries and consulted sections appear in the research library's literature notes and the comparative essay.

Research method

Each case pairs an object or process with dated geographical evidence. The source registers identify the material consulted, its locator, and limits on interpretation. The investigation distinguishes original records, modern historical interpretation, archival finding aids and teaching reconstructions. Six case studies support comparison; they do not form a representative sample of national manufacturing.

The most consequential checks concern the difference between a retail advertisement and a shipment record, a corporate address and a factory, an expansion forecast and completed capacity, and a period handbook and a named firm's operating procedure. The source registers retain these distinctions so that readers can examine the basis for each claim.
