# Where Manufacturing Happens: Concentration and Distributed Work in Six Objects

## Introduction

A finished object offers a misleadingly simple answer to where manufacturing happens: the place where its parts finally come together. This project challenges that answer through six historical snapshots: a nail around 1650, cloth in 1835, canned meat in 1901, a Ford Model A in 1928, an RCA television in 1965, and an iPhone in 2007. Its argument is that concentrating production repeatedly reorganized geographically distributed work rather than eliminating it. Transport enabled connections, but corporate ownership, specialized capabilities, and decisions about coordination shaped their organization. The resulting atlas therefore questions both the self-sufficient factory and an inevitable progression from local production to globalization.

## Background

The course lecture *American Empire and Industrialization* by Marco Gabriell Muñoz connects industrialization to regional inequality and concentrated capital (Muñoz, 2026, Lecture 4, slides 4, 6–7). Its discussion of northern investment in southern industry distinguishes the location of productive activity from the location of economic control; its Gilded Age discussion connects industrial expansion to changes in work and wealth. The atlas develops that distinction at the scale of individual products. Its maps separate raw materials, processing, assembly, and corporate locations. These are analytical categories, not interchangeable dots. A generated component study explains selected structural relationships, while a geographical map asks where documented tasks occurred. Neither visual device independently establishes a historical supply chain. The comparison also draws on Lecture 3’s discussion of sectional identity and slavery (slides 4–6), Lecture 5’s account of automobiles and manufacturing in the 1920s (slide 10), and Lecture 6’s treatment of globalization (slide 19). These lectures establish the historical questions; outside sources establish the selected objects’ production arrangements.

## Argument

Early production already combined concentrated processing with dispersed work. At Saugus, iron passed through a furnace, forge, and rolling and slitting mill. Most nail rods were shipped to Boston and other New England settlements, although some became nails locally (National Park Service, 2021). The nail around 1650 thus anchors a colonial production system, not a claim about a traceable surviving specimen. Its significance lies in the distinction between making a standardized intermediate material and finishing a usable object. A specialized works could concentrate demanding operations while supplying other producers. Local manufacture did not require every stage to remain local.

The 1835 cloth case shows that geographical separation could also conceal social dependence. Lowell Manufacturing Company produced inexpensive cloth for sale to enslavers, while Lowell mills relied on cotton produced through enslaved labor (National Park Service, 2020). The northern factory and southern plantation consequently belonged to connected economic relationships. This does not establish a closed circuit in which one identifiable batch of cotton returned as clothing. It establishes a narrower but consequential point: a northern manufacturing location did not make production independent of southern coercion. Here, identifying the source of raw material changes the interpretation of the finished object.

The canned-meat case shifts the question from separated stages to concentrated processing. A catalog records a 1901 film of can labeling at Armour's Chicago operation (American Film Institute, n.d.); this is evidence of a documented activity, not a basis for reconstructing unseen footage. Chicago's stockyards brought livestock by rail into an urban processing system with specialized jobs (Chicago History Museum, n.d.). Rail connections helped make concentration possible, but a junction alone does not explain why production accumulated there. Klein and Crafts (2009) find that market potential, interacting with scale economies and industrial linkages, helps explain the manufacturing belt. Their regional analysis suggests a competing mechanism: factories could benefit from access to customers and other producers as well as incoming materials. It does not independently prove Armour's specific location decisions.

The Model A illustrates why factory concentration must be distinguished from territorial self-sufficiency. A dated photograph records Model A assembly at Ford's Rouge complex in February 1928 (Ford Motor Company, Photographic Department, 1928). The Henry Ford's railroad history explains that Rouge obtained coal from Kentucky and West Virginia and connected external transport with internal materials handling (The Henry Ford, n.d.). The important boundary was therefore organizational as well as geographical. Bringing operations into a large complex did not bring their resource base inside its walls. Nor does the complex's later development establish that every component was already manufactured there in 1928.

The RCA and iPhone cases reveal a further distinction between distributing work across company plants and coordinating external manufacturers. A 1965 industry report identifies RCA's tube facilities at Marion and Lancaster, receiver production at Bloomington, and components at Indianapolis. It also describes proposed expansion, which must not be mistaken for completed capacity (Broadcasting, 1965, pp. 28–29). Apple's 2007 annual report, by contrast, places final assembly of substantially all its portable products, explicitly including the iPhone, with third-party vendors in China (Apple Inc., 2007, pp. 12, 16–17). These primary sources show different organizational arrangements without requiring an unsupported factory location for every component.

Reading those two sources together also reveals how evidence shapes the apparent geography of production. The trade report presents RCA's network through expansion plans and anticipated demand; Apple's legally required disclosure emphasizes dependence and disruption risk (Broadcasting, 1965, pp. 28–29; Apple Inc., 2007, pp. 16–17). The RCA forecast is “entirely dependent upon” picture-tube availability (p. 28), making projected expansion conditional on a specific component. Apple, meanwhile, says outsourcing arrangements “reduce the Company’s direct control” (p. 16), placing a governance cost alongside potential savings. Expansion and vulnerability are not opposite conditions: both presuppose coordination among specialized locations. Yet their different purposes prevent a simple comparison of corporate success. Gereffi et al. (2003) explain why coordination depends on transaction complexity, the ability to codify information, and supplier capabilities. This framework sharpens the comparison beyond “American” versus “foreign” manufacturing. It does not authorize assigning every Apple relationship to one governance category from a company address or a component label.

## Counterarguments

A reasonable objection is that transport improvement provides a simpler explanation for the atlas's widening connections. Transport matters, but it cannot alone explain why operations cluster or why firms own some stages and outsource others. Klein and Crafts (2009) distinguish market access and industrial linkages from natural advantages, while Gereffi et al. (2003) show that similar geographical separation can accommodate different forms of coordination. Together, these explanations make distance a condition to investigate rather than a sufficient cause. The six cases also form a selective comparison, not a representative sample from which to calculate national manufacturing shares.

Continuity across the cases should not erase historical change. A colonial nail-rod network and an electronics supply chain differ substantially in technology, scale, institutions, and the tasks being coordinated. The claim is therefore about a recurring relationship between concentration and distribution, not an unchanged system. A dated epilogue makes its continuing relevance visible: Apple's 2025 report states that changing its supply chain requires considerable time and resources (Apple Inc., 2025, Item 1A). That contemporary company-level statement supports the importance of established relationships; it supplies no missing evidence about the 2007 iPhone's component origins.

## Conclusion

The atlas's contribution is to make a factory location the beginning of historical explanation rather than its endpoint. Across six objects, concentrated operations appear within wider arrangements of materials, labor, transport, and authority. Comparing those arrangements connects small material objects to the course's larger questions about regional development and capital. The maps and diagrams support that argument only when they distinguish documented connections from schematic reconstruction. Manufacturing geography becomes most revealing when it shows both what a place does and what that activity depends upon elsewhere.
