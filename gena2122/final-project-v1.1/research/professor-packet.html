Comparative essay

Where Work Happens and Who Connects It

Introduction

The geography of American manufacturing is better understood by distinguishing where work occurred from who controlled its connections. Across these six cases, ownership, exchange, contracts and coercive labor relations crossed production locations in different ways; distance alone does not describe a progression from local work to globalization.

Made Across America tests this argument through six dated cases. Each complicates a simple reading of production: Saugus challenges an entirely local colonial economy; Lowell separates northern industrial identity from economic independence from slavery; Armour distinguishes a processing center from a product’s untraced retail origin; Ford separates integration from territorial self-sufficiency; RCA shows company plants alongside outside sales; and the iPhone separates a US brand from domestic manufacture. The title concerns American participation in manufacturing, including connections beyond US borders. These are selected cases, not a representative sample or six equally documented snapshots.

Course context and creative method

Lecture 4’s account of northern capital in southern industry implies a distinction between productive activity and economic control (Muñoz, 2026, slide 4). The exhibition develops that question through objects. Selecting a task highlights components and places; an accompanying relationship label identifies ownership, trade, labor coercion, contracts or an unresolved connection. The comparison asks readers to reconsider a place after learning how it was linked to others. Generated components explain structure, while sources establish historical claims. The GDP panel supplies dated national context, not a measure of these networks’ concentration or economic effects.

Six cases

At Saugus around 1650, demanding iron processing took place at one works, while most nail rods went to Boston and other New England settlements (National Park Service, 2021a). English investment and colonial privileges helped organize the venture; a local-supply condition qualified its export freedom. The works nevertheless ceased operating around 1670 amid mismanagement and litigation (Regan & White, n.d., pp.27,32). This unusual, ultimately unsuccessful venture cannot stand for all colonial production. It brings Lecture 2’s colonial commercial relations into a specific institution: local processing could depend on overseas capital and public privileges. Scottish prisoners worked under indenture, including in woodcutting and charcoal making (National Park Service, 2021b). Materials and labor were secured through different relationships.

The 1835 Lowell case makes slavery a condition of the network, rather than incidental background. Lowell mills used southern cotton produced through enslaved labor; Lowell Manufacturing Company also made cloth sold to enslavers (National Park Service, 2020). A compilation dated 1835 by NPS places a racialized clothing category alongside household textiles. The table records commercial classification; the historical interpretation establishes the sales relationship. The particular clothing’s fiber composition and any same-bale return journey remain unknown. Lecture 3’s sectional contrast is therefore complicated by economic interdependence: locating a mill in the North did not separate its business from slavery. This was an actively maintained commercial relationship, not necessarily hidden from its participants.

Armour requires a composite evidence window. An 1893 Vacaville advertisement names Armour Corned Beef; a catalog records a 1901 film of labeling in Armour’s Chicago Canned Meat Department (J. & I. Blum, 1893; American Film Institute, n.d.). The latter is not specifically a record of the advertised beef. Chicago’s industry received Midwestern livestock by rail and concentrated specialized processing jobs (Clark, n.d., pp.1–2). These observations connect Lecture 4’s railways, capital and labor to a processing center, while leaving the advertised cans’ factory and contracting chain unresolved. Regional supply and distant retail presence are evidence of different relationships, not a verified shipment between the two points.

The Model A case asks what integration actually joined. A February 1928 photograph records assembly at Rouge; museum accounts describe regional coal supply and wooden body parts routed through Iron Mountain and body suppliers (Ford Motor Company, Photographic Department, 1928; The Henry Ford, n.d.-a; Anderson, n.d.). The strongest counterreading is that vertical integration did internalize substantial coordination. That is compatible with resources and company facilities outside Rouge, and with external suppliers. Work inside one site, ownership across sites and purchases from other firms must be distinguished. Richmond’s separate Model A assembly plant opened in 1931, beyond the map’s 1928 anchor (National Park Service, n.d.). Lecture 5’s automotive economy thus becomes a question about corporate organization rather than proof that one enormous factory did everything.

RCA’s June 1965 plans directed $36.4 million of a $50-million program toward picture-tube expansion. Lancaster already made rectangular tubes; Marion was to be equipped for them. The company also sold tubes to competing receiver makers (Broadcasting, 1965, pp.28–29). This industry already combined internal production with trade between firms. Its capital-plan report reveals selected RCA facilities, but cannot establish what RCA bought elsewhere. The report’s industry sales estimate was “entirely dependent upon” tube availability (p.28): the conditional figure concerned sales, not whether expansion could occur. Management’s reduced 1965 tube forecast during changeover and its limits on competitors’ requests identify capacity and allocation as separate problems.

Apple’s 2007 disclosure gives concentration another meaning. It places final assembly of substantially all portable products, including iPhone, with third-party vendors in China, while describing outsourced manufacturing as concentrated among few manufacturers and often single locations (Apple Inc., 2007, pp.12,16). The same company-wide report lists its own Cork facility and US production relationships; those are not iPhone-specific locations. RCA and Apple therefore do not establish a clean transition from internal production to outsourcing. Their sources disclose different slices of mixed arrangements. Apple’s warning that outsourcing can “reduce the Company’s direct control” (p.16) makes contractual dependence explicit. The packaging also tells a selective American story: Apple and California receive named design credit, while the assembly statement names China without naming the assembler (The Henry Ford, n.d.-b).

Competing explanations and limits

Transport remains part of the explanation. Klein and Crafts (2009) compare market potential and factor endowments across states and two-digit industries; their account includes scale economies relative to transport costs (pp.15,24,28–29). Agricultural inputs remain a significant natural-advantage channel through 1900. Their discussion also identifies food as an exception in cited aggregate scale-economy evidence. The study therefore prompts questions about both livestock and market access, rather than proving an Armour-specific mechanism or defeating a transport explanation. Gereffi et al. (2003) provide an analytical framework for asking how transaction complexity, codification and supplier capability affect coordination. Their types are tools for inquiry, not classifications established by this exhibition’s map points.

Cowie offers a stronger challenge to an account centered on technical capacity: RCA’s assembly relocations pursued cheaper, more controllable labor as established workers developed bargaining power (n.d., pp.4–6,9). The consulted management report cannot adjudicate that explanation. His account includes gendered labor markets, acknowledged here without opening another research strand. He dates Mexican employment to 1968 and treats crossing borders as both continuity and a consequential change in industrial relations (p.2). This qualifies a reading of Lecture 6’s globalization context that would begin the process with 1990s agreements. Neither the iPhone records nor a supplier-office map can settle the weight of wages in assembly location.

Selection and source genre also limit the argument. Cases with surviving external links are especially visible; no prevalence estimate follows. The interpretation would need revision if plant or contract records disproved the attributed relationships. A competing account could place national regulation or labor conditions ahead of corporate coordination; resolving that ranking would require evidence beyond the selected cases. The 2025 afterword keeps the question current: Apple again describes concentrated supply chains, single-source partners and reduced direct control (2025, Item 1A). These company-wide statements support comparison with the 2007 risk, not a reconstruction of the original phone using later suppliers.

Conclusion

The exhibition’s contribution is to place a second question beside every production location: what connected this work to other places? The answer varies from colonial privileges and coerced labor to ownership, sales and supplier contracts, with gaps left visible. This approach connects small objects to the course’s account of regional development and to its question of who tells American stories. It preserves the significance of borders and institutions while resisting the idea that a larger map alone explains historical change.

Exhibition text

The following six texts match the website. Source codes refer to the indexed bibliography below.

1650 — The iron nail

At Saugus in the Massachusetts Bay Colony, concentrated processing supplied work beyond the ironworks. Bog ore, charcoal and flux from Nahant entered production; refining, rolling and slitting produced wrought-iron bars and nail rods. Scottish prisoners of war also labored under indenture, including in woodcutting and charcoal making. [S1, S3, S6, S8]

Most nail rods went to Boston and other New England settlements, although some nails were made at Saugus. Their movement connected demanding mill operations with dispersed finishing. English investment and colonial privileges helped organize the venture: a monopoly agreement required local needs to take priority over exports. [S1, S4, p. 32]

The works demonstrate productive coordination, not lasting commercial success. Regan and White link their closure around 1670 to mismanagement and litigation. The surviving record therefore reveals both what this arrangement could produce and how it could fail. The nail represents that network around 1650, before the United States existed. [S4, p. 27]

Course connection. Lecture 2, pp. 12–13 frames colonial commercial ties. Saugus complicates a simple trade map: English investment and colonial rules organized local production, while success remained uncertain. Lecture 1, pp. 25–26 helps ask what a map of materials leaves out, including labor obligations.

Connection type. Investment · colonial rules · indenture — English investment and colonial privileges supported local ironwork; Scottish prisoners supplied some labor under indenture. Distribution beyond Saugus did not mean one labor or ownership regime. [S1, S4, S8]

1835 — Cotton cloth

Lowell mills relied on southern cotton produced through enslaved labor. Waterpower and investment concentrated processing at Lowell; the Middlesex Canal carried goods. The railway opened to the public in 1835, but an 1836 guide reported merchandise arrangements still in progress, alongside small private-car shipments. Opening a line did not establish a completed freight transition. [L1, L3, L6, L7]

An 1835 compilation, dated by the National Park Service, lists the Lowell company’s goods as “Carpet, Rugs and Negro Cloth.” The racialized category sits alongside household textiles as an ordinary commercial entry. NPS identifies clothing made for sale to enslavers; the table alone does not identify its purchasers. [L2, L3]

These connections complicate a simple division between a manufacturing North and an agricultural South. The illustration explains cotton processing. The specific fiber composition of the clothing sold to enslavers remains unconfirmed, and no identifiable cotton batch is traced south again as clothing.

Course connection. Lecture 3, slides 4–6 contrasts northern and southern identities. The 1835 textile case shows economic dependence across that division; it is not evidence about factory totals at the later outbreak of war. Lecture 4, slide 4 provides a later comparison with southern textile industrialization.

Connection type. Commodity trade · enslaved labor — Mill purchases connected northern processing with cotton produced through enslaved labor; cloth sales to enslavers added a market relationship. Trade does not make the labor behind it voluntary. [L2, L3]

1901 — Canned beef

Chicago's meatpacking industry concentrated processing where railways brought livestock from the Midwest. Specialized work around the Union Stock Yard connected regional supply with an urban processing center. The evidence describes an industry, not the origin of every Armour can. [M4]

An 1893 Vacaville, California advertisement offered Armour Corned Beef. An AFI catalog records can labeling in Armour's Chicago Canned Meat Department in 1901. These are separate observations: a distant retail market and a documented processing activity. The advertised cans' manufacturing location remains unknown. [M1, M2]

Schwaab's 1890 handbook presents preservation and easier transportation as commercial advantages, then describes cooking, deboning and packing corned beef. It explains why canning could serve distant markets, without supplying an Armour recipe or a traced shipment. The illustrated metal container's supplier is also unknown. [M5, pp. 112–113]

Course connection. Lecture 4, slides 6–7 links rail expansion, concentrated capital and industrial labor. Chicago shows how these conditions could combine at a processing center; the Vacaville advertisement adds a distinct retail observation. Slide 15's food reforms belong to a later period, not the 1901 film's cause.

Connection type. Processing · retail origin unknown — Chicago activity and Vacaville retail are separately documented. Their contract or shipment connection is unknown, as is the advertised cans’ manufacturing origin. [M1, M2, M4]

1928 — The Model A

A photograph dated February 8, 1928 records Model A assembly at Ford's Rouge complex. Integration brought metallurgy, component production and final assembly into one large site. It also extended through Ford-owned facilities elsewhere and relationships with suppliers. Organizational control and physical concentration were different achievements. [F1, F4, F5]

The museum's histories locate coal supplies in Kentucky and West Virginia and describe Iron Mountain's wooden body parts passing through body suppliers. These selected connections explain work beyond Rouge; they are not a complete list of 1928 suppliers. A separate Model A assembly plant opened in Richmond, California in 1931, outside this map's 1928 snapshot. [F3, F4, F9]

Dates further limit reconstruction. An archive chronology places standard safety glass in November 1928; a factory history confirms Rouge's own safety-glass production by 1930. Neither establishes the glass source for the February car. The component illustration represents major Model A systems, not a recovered factory sequence. [F5, F7, p. 40]

Course connection. Lecture 5, slide 10 places automobiles within 1920s manufacturing. Rouge shows how corporate control could integrate geographically separated work, rather than proving that every operation occupied one site. Slide 12 supplies Great Migration context, not the identities of photographed workers.

Connection type. Company facilities · outside suppliers — Ford combined Rouge operations with facilities elsewhere and body-supplier relationships. An input outside Dearborn was not necessarily outside company ownership; ownership is not assigned to every mapped source. [F3–F5]

1965 — Color television

RCA's 1965 color-television business combined specialized plants. Bloomington made receivers and Indianapolis supplied components. Lancaster already made rectangular picture tubes; Marion was to be equipped for them. This is company-capacity evidence, not the supply history of the illustrated set. [R1, pp. 28–29]

Broadcasting reported $36.4 million for tube expansion within a $50-million program. RCA also reduced its 1965 tube estimate from 1.5 million to 1.35 million during the production changeover. Management said it could not meet all competing set makers' requests. The bottleneck concerned capacity and allocation, not simply more assembly space. These were reported plans and forecasts. [R1, pp. 28–29]

Existing technical capacity offers one explanation for expansion. Cowie's assembly history poses another: firms sought cheaper, more controllable labor as relations changed at established sites. Management's expansion report cannot settle that explanation. The cabinet, tube, chassis and speaker visualize tasks; they do not assign every part to a factory. [R2, R5, pp. 4–6, 9]

Course connection. Lecture 6, slide 8 frames postwar regional change, while slide 19 discusses NAFTA-era globalization. Cowie dates RCA employment in Mexico to 1968 and traces earlier US relocations: the case complicates a story that begins cross-border production in the 1990s. These dates are outside research, not lecture claims.

Connection type. Company plants · sales to competitors — RCA allocated investment among its own plants and supplied tubes to competing receiver makers. Its capital-plan report does not inventory RCA’s outside purchases. [R1, p.29]

2007 — The first iPhone

The original iPhone's packaging names Apple and California for design, while identifying China for assembly. This is also a corporate account of who contributes what: Apple is named; assemblers are not. The 2007 annual report confirms third-party assembly in China for portable products including iPhone, without identifying a particular plant. [I1, I4]

A 2007 8GB teardown, consulted in a later-revised version, identifies specialized components. The optional office map includes four suppliers matched to period company records. Wolfson, Linear Technology and Skyworks were also identified but remain unmapped because equivalent location checks were not completed. No office establishes a component's factory. [I3, I6–I9]

Apple coordinated manufacturing across firms while acknowledging reduced direct control. The evidence establishes that arrangement; it cannot decide how much wages influenced assembly location. Company-wide US manufacturing disclosures cannot be assigned to this phone. Its bonded display, upper board, solder-connected lower battery and rear enclosure are shown as structural relationships, not factory steps. [I3, I4, I10]

Course connection. Lecture 6, slide 19 supplies a globalization framework. The packaging adds a question central to the course: who tells an American product's story, and whose contribution is named? The 2007 case compares corporate organization with physical manufacture; a separately dated 2025 afterword does not alter its map.

Connection type. Third-party manufacturing · contracts — Apple disclosed reliance on a few manufacturers and often single locations, with reduced direct production control. These company-wide risks do not locate every iPhone supplier. [I4, p.16]

Source analysis

The argument depends on comparing what sources can establish, as well as what they leave unresolved. The following examples are part of the research method, rather than a claim to have consulted every original record in the referenced archives.

A product table and a historical interpretation

The Lowell column in a compilation dated 1835 by NPS places “Carpet, Rugs and Negro Cloth” in one product cell. Its arrangement records a racialized clothing category alongside household goods as ordinary business output. Another row mentions both cotton and wool, so total yardage cannot identify the composition of that particular clothing category. NPS's historical interpretation establishes sales to enslavers; the table's product names do not identify buyers on their own. Neither source traces a bale back south as clothing. [L2, L3]

The railway evidence offers a second close-reading test. The 1836 guide, as quoted in the 1885 history and transcribed by UMass Lowell, separates the opening of public service from still-developing merchandise arrangements. That distinction changes the map: a new railway connection cannot simply be equated with a completed transfer of cotton freight from the canal. [L7]

An advertisement and an industrial catalog record

The Vacaville advertisement of 28 January 1893 is a contemporary attempt to sell goods to a local audience. Its evidentiary value here is the named product's presence in a distant retail market. It provides no shipment itinerary or sales total. The AFI entry for Labeling Cans records a 1901 film associated with Armour's Chicago operation. The catalog was consulted; the film was not viewed. It would therefore be misleading to present a detailed account of workers' movements as an observation from the film. [M1, M2]

Schwaab’s handbook connects preservation with convenient transportation, then advocates expanding exports (pp.112–113). The commercial argument gives a reason why canning could serve distant markets; it does not demonstrate the route of the advertised Armour product. The container’s metal supplier remains unknown. [M5]

A company photograph

Ford's Photographic Department recorded a Model A assembly line at Rouge on 8 February 1928. The image and catalog identify an activity, location and date. They do not by themselves reveal the origins of every component or the workers' personal histories. External supply relationships therefore depend on additional material in The Henry Ford's railroad and factory histories. This separation matters because distant resources may be company-owned or purchased: their location alone does not determine the organizational boundary. The 1924 advertising campaign is used through the museum’s interpretation, not as a claimed close reading of the original advertisement. [F1, F3, F5]

Expansion reporting and corporate risk disclosure

Broadcasting reported RCA's color-television expansion on 21 June 1965 through an industry perspective attentive to demand, production and executive forecasts. The report locates specialized activities but also communicates expectations. Apple's 2007 Form 10-K addresses investors through a legally required account of its business and risks. Its discussion of third-party manufacturing makes dependency visible in a different way. Reading the documents together supports a comparison of coordination arrangements; it cannot establish that their maps measure the same kind of facility or that one firm was more successful. [R1; I4]

The RCA report qualifies its industry sales forecast as “entirely dependent upon” picture-tube availability (p. 28). The dependency clause makes a component constraint central to an optimistic expansion story: projected sales are conditional, not an observed result. Apple's 2007 report pairs possible cost savings with “reduce the Company’s direct control” (p. 16). That wording identifies a trade-off within outsourcing rather than treating international distance itself as the risk. Read together, the passages support the argument that specialized production requires coordination, while retaining the distinction between a forecast and a risk disclosure. [R1; I4]

The reported investment and tube forecast make the constraint measurable: $36.4 million of a $50-million program went toward tube expansion, while the 1965 tube estimate fell from 1.5 million to 1.35 million during changeover. Management also explained limits on supplies to competitors. These are attributed contemporary claims about plans and allocation, not independently observed final results. Lancaster’s existing rectangular-tube production and Marion’s announced tooling must remain distinct. [R1, p.29]

A label assigns credit

The iPhone packaging names Apple in California for design but identifies only China for assembly. Reading that difference as corporate attribution brings the course’s question about who tells an American story into the object itself. It does not establish a legal reason for the wording, the value of each task, or the identity of the assemblers. Apple’s company-wide US production disclosures cannot be reassigned to this phone. [I1; I4, p.12]

Secondary research as a competing explanation

Klein and Crafts’s 2009 working paper compares market potential with factor endowments in state-by-industry data. Transport costs form part of its agglomeration mechanism; agricultural inputs and the aggregate food-industry exception limit its application to meatpacking. It prompts questions about both material and market access rather than proving Armour’s decisions. Gereffi, Humphrey and Sturgeon's 2003 manuscript examines how transaction complexity, codification and supplier capabilities shape coordination. It makes the distinction between RCA and Apple more precise, while leaving individual relationships unclassified when evidence is insufficient. Cowie’s Capital Moves introduction challenges static location factors by placing changing labor control at the center of RCA’s assembly relocation. Its comparison is an explanatory alternative, not merely background; it does not settle why the separate tube plants expanded. The recorded reading covers pp.1–9, not the whole book.

These secondary works are used for their explanatory alternatives and limits. Their full bibliographic entries and consulted sections appear in the research library's literature notes and the comparative essay.

Comparison across cases

The columns compare tasks and evidence, not the size or superiority of whole economies. A blank or uncertain dimension is not a zero. The sequence is selective: geography does not supply an outcome measure by itself.

Saugus, c.1650 Near-area ore, charcoal and flux; centralized refining/slitting; rods finished elsewhere [S1, S3, S6] English and colonial investment; colonial privilege and local-supply rules [S4] Scottish prisoners worked under indenture in tasks including woodcutting and charcoal making; not a workforce census [S8] New England nail-rod distribution; broader iron-goods trade crossed the Atlantic, without a traced nail route [S1, S4]

The early comparison already prevents a single-direction story: Saugus combined nearby material inputs with Atlantic capital and broader trade. A later case can have longer raw-material links without having a more international capital network. The later cases instead sharpen a question of ownership and coordination—what work is brought inside a firm, and what remains dependent on other places and organizations?
